## Page 03 — What is Financial Accounting? - Definition
### Original Page Content
**Financial accounting** is the process of **recording, classifying, summarizing, and reporting** the financial transactions of a business to provide useful information for decision-making.

### Visual Explanation
A decorative accounting illustration shows charts, a calculator, financial reports, and a dollar sign. It reinforces that financial accounting converts business transactions into organized information for analysis and decisions.

### Explanation
A simple flow is:

```text
Transactions
   ↓
Record
   ↓
Classify
   ↓
Summarize
   ↓
Report
   ↓
Decision-making
```

### Definition
**Definition:** Financial accounting is the process of recording, classifying, summarizing, and reporting financial transactions to provide useful information for decision-making.

**Simple meaning:** It turns day-to-day financial transactions into structured reports people can use to understand a business.

### Exam Tip
Memorize the four action words: **recording → classifying → summarizing → reporting**.

### Common Mistake
No major common mistake.

### Likely Exam Questions
1. **Q:** Define financial accounting.  
   **A:** The process of recording, classifying, summarizing, and reporting business financial transactions to provide useful decision-making information.

## Page 04 — What is Financial Accounting? - Purpose and Users
### Original Page Content
- Financial accounting prepares key financial statements (**Balance Sheet, Income Statement, and Cash Flow Statement**) for external users such as **investors and lenders**.
- These statements help users understand a company's **financial position, performance, and cash flow**.
- It provides a structured way to communicate financial information.
- Financial data supports decisions such as **project investments, cost management, and evaluating business viability**.

### Visual Explanation
The visual shows three labeled icons: **Balance Sheet**, **Income Statement**, and **Cash Flow Statement**, emphasizing the three key reports produced through financial accounting.

### Explanation
The three statements answer different questions:

| Statement | Main question |
|---|---|
| Balance Sheet | What does the business own and owe now? |
| Income Statement | Did the business make a profit over the period? |
| Cash Flow Statement | Where did cash come from and where did it go? |

### Exam Tip
Do not treat the three statements as interchangeable. Link each statement to **position**, **performance**, or **cash flow**.

### Common Mistake
Confusing profit with cash. A company may report profit but still have cash-flow problems.

### Likely Exam Questions
1. **Q:** Name the three key financial statements.  
   **A:** Balance Sheet, Income Statement, and Cash Flow Statement.
2. **Q:** Give two external users of financial accounting information.  
   **A:** Investors and lenders.

<a id="page-06---revenue-income"></a>
## Page 06 — Revenue & Income
### Original Page Content
**Revenue**  
The total money earned from selling products or services **before** any expenses are deducted. Also called the **"top line."**

**Income**  
What remains after subtracting expenses from revenue. Often used interchangeably with **"profit."** Also called the **"bottom line."**

### Visual Explanation
Two dark information panels compare Revenue and Income. Their side-by-side positioning highlights that they are related but not the same concept.

### Explanation
The key difference is timing in the calculation:
- Revenue is earned **before expenses**.
- Income/profit is what remains **after expenses**.

### Definition
**Definition - Revenue:** Total money earned from selling products or services before expenses.

**Simple meaning:** Sales/earnings before costs are removed.

**Definition - Income:** What remains after subtracting expenses from revenue.

**Simple meaning:** The bottom-line amount left after costs.

### Exam Tip
Keyword memory: **Revenue = top line; Income/Profit = bottom line.**

### Common Mistake
Using 'revenue' and 'profit' as if they are always the same. Revenue is before expenses; profit/income is after expenses.

### Likely Exam Questions
1. **Q:** Differentiate revenue and income.  
   **A:** Revenue is total earnings before expenses; income is the amount remaining after expenses are deducted.

## Page 07 — Expenses & Expenditure
### Original Page Content
**Expenses**  
Recurring costs required to keep the business running - salaries, rent, utilities, and supplies.

**Expenditure**  
All money spent by the business. The terms overlap, but not all spending is an expense - buying equipment is an **investment**, not a daily expense.

### Visual Explanation
The slide uses two comparison boxes, one for recurring operating expenses and one for broader expenditure.

### Explanation
**Expenses** are a subset of business spending. **Expenditure** is broader. A large equipment purchase is money spent, but it is treated differently from normal recurring operating expenses.

### Definition
**Definition - Expense:** A recurring cost required to keep the business running.

**Simple meaning:** Regular operating cost.

**Definition - Expenditure:** All money spent by the business.

**Simple meaning:** Any business spending, whether operating or investment-related.

### Exam Tip
When asked to classify spending, ask: **Is this a recurring operating cost or a broader/investment purchase?**

### Common Mistake
Assuming every expenditure is immediately an expense. The slide specifically notes equipment as an investment rather than a daily expense.

<a id="page-08---profit-loss"></a>
## Page 08 — Profit & Loss
### Original Page Content
**Formula from the slide:**

`Revenue - Expenses = Profit (negative result = Loss)`

**Profit:** Revenue exceeds expenses. The business keeps the difference.

**Loss:** Expenses exceed revenue. The business is "in the red."

### Visual Explanation
The slide visually contrasts Profit and Loss in separate panels and highlights that a negative result from Revenue - Expenses represents a loss.

### Explanation
```text
If Revenue > Expenses  → Profit
If Revenue < Expenses  → Loss
```

### Definition
**Definition - Profit:** The positive difference when revenue exceeds expenses.

**Definition - Loss:** The negative result when expenses exceed revenue.

### Exam Tip
Write the formula first: **Profit = Revenue - Expenses**. A negative answer means **Loss**.

### Common Mistake
Subtracting revenue from expenses instead of expenses from revenue.

### Likely Exam Questions
1. **Q:** Revenue is LKR 500,000 and expenses are LKR 420,000. Profit or loss?  
   **A:** Profit of LKR 80,000.

<a id="page-11---equity"></a>
## Page 11 — Equity
### Original Page Content
**Equity** represents the owner's stake in the business - what would remain if all debts were paid off.

**What Equity Includes**
- Owner's contributions - money invested into the business
- Retained earnings - accumulated profits reinvested over time
- Shareholder value - in corporations, equity is divided into shares

**Equation:** `Equity = Assets - Liabilities`

**Example:** A business with $100K in assets and $30K in debt holds $70K in equity.

### Visual Explanation
The main visual emphasis is the equation **Equity = Assets - Liabilities**, which connects the three Balance Sheet categories.

### Explanation
Equity is the residual interest after liabilities are deducted from assets.

### Definition
**Definition:** Equity is the owner's residual stake in the business after liabilities are deducted from assets.

**Simple meaning:** What belongs to the owners after all debts are paid.

### Exam Tip
Memorize both forms: **Equity = Assets - Liabilities** and **Assets = Liabilities + Equity**.

### Common Mistake
Treating equity as cash. Equity is an ownership claim/residual value, not necessarily cash held in the bank.

### Likely Exam Questions
1. **Q:** Assets = LKR 100,000 and liabilities = LKR 30,000. Find equity.  
   **A:** LKR 70,000.

<a id="page-15---cash-flow"></a>
## Page 15 — Cash Flow
### Original Page Content
**Positive Cash Flow**  
More money coming in than going out. The business can pay its bills, invest, and grow sustainably.

**Negative Cash Flow**  
More money going out than coming in. A business can be profitable on paper yet still go bankrupt without available cash.

### Visual Explanation
The two-box comparison contrasts positive and negative cash flow. This visual reinforces that the direction of cash movement has practical consequences for business survival.

### Explanation
Profit and cash are not the same thing. A company may record accounting profit but still face cash shortages if cash is not available when obligations fall due.

### Definition
**Definition - Positive Cash Flow:** Cash inflows exceed cash outflows.

**Definition - Negative Cash Flow:** Cash outflows exceed cash inflows.

### Exam Tip
A common theory question is: **Can a profitable company still fail?** Yes - if it runs out of cash.

### Common Mistake
Assuming profit automatically means positive cash flow.

### Likely Exam Questions
1. **Q:** Why can a profitable company still go bankrupt?  
   **A:** It may not have enough available cash to meet obligations despite reporting profit.

## Page 18 — Interpreting the Balance Sheet
### Original Page Content
The Balance Sheet provides insights into:
- **Financial position:** what the business owns and owes at a specific point in time.
- **Liquidity:** comparing current assets and current liabilities to assess ability to meet short-term obligations.
- **Financial structure:** whether the business is financed through debt (liabilities) or owner's funds (equity).
- **Solvency and risk:** higher debt relative to equity may indicate greater financial risk.
- **Resource allocation:** how resources are invested in assets to support operations.

**In simple terms:** The Balance Sheet helps users evaluate the company's stability, risk level, and ability to meet financial obligations.

### Visual Explanation
No major visual content; this is an interpretation-focused slide.

### Explanation
A Balance Sheet is not just a list of numbers. Interpretation asks what those numbers imply about **liquidity, financing, risk, and resource use**.

### Exam Tip
For an interpretation answer, use keywords: **position, liquidity, structure, solvency/risk, resource allocation**.

### Common Mistake
Judging financial health from total assets alone without considering liabilities and liquidity.

### Likely Exam Questions
1. **Q:** How can a Balance Sheet be used to assess liquidity?  
   **A:** Compare current assets with current liabilities to judge ability to meet short-term obligations.

<a id="page-20---profit-levels"></a>
## Page 20 — Profit Levels
### Original Page Content
Profit levels are different stages at which profit is calculated in the Income Statement.

| Profit level | Calculation / meaning |
|---|---|
| **Gross Profit** | Revenue minus Cost of Sales; shows efficiency in producing/delivering products or services |
| **Operating Profit** | Gross Profit minus Operating Expenses; reflects profit from core business operations |
| **Net Profit** | Final profit after deducting all expenses, including finance costs and taxes |

These levels help identify where profits are generated or reduced.

**In simple terms:** Profit levels break total profit into stages to analyze operational efficiency and overall profitability.

### Visual Explanation
Three horizontally aligned boxes show Gross Profit → Operating Profit → Net Profit, making the step-by-step reduction of profit visually clear.

### Explanation
```text
Revenue
  - Cost of Sales
= Gross Profit
  - Operating Expenses
= Operating Profit
  - Remaining expenses (including finance costs and taxes)
= Net Profit
```

### Definition
**Definition - Gross Profit:** Revenue minus Cost of Sales.

**Definition - Operating Profit:** Gross Profit minus Operating Expenses.

**Definition - Net Profit:** Final profit after all expenses, including finance costs and taxes.

### Exam Tip
High-value calculation area. Know **Gross Profit** and **Operating Profit** formulas exactly.

### Common Mistake
Subtracting operating expenses when calculating Gross Profit. Operating expenses are deducted after Gross Profit to obtain Operating Profit.

### Likely Exam Questions

# Full Lecture Summary

## 1. Purpose of Financial Accounting
Financial accounting records, classifies, summarizes, and reports transactions so that users can make informed decisions. It communicates financial information through structured statements and supports decisions such as investment, cost management, and evaluating business viability.

## 2. Core Financial Concepts
- **Revenue:** total earnings before expenses.
- **Income/Profit:** amount remaining after expenses.
- **Expenses:** recurring costs of running the business.
- **Expenditure:** all business spending; not every expenditure is a daily expense.
- **Assets:** resources of value owned/controlled by the business.
- **Liabilities:** financial obligations owed to outsiders.
- **Equity:** owners' residual stake after liabilities are deducted from assets.
- **Cash flow:** movement of cash into and out of the business.

## 3. Three Key Financial Statements

| Statement | Main purpose | Time perspective | Main items |
|---|---|---|---|
| Balance Sheet | Financial position | Specific point in time | Assets, liabilities, equity |
| Income Statement | Financial performance | Over a period | Revenue, expenses, profit/loss |
| Cash Flow Statement | Cash movement/liquidity | Over a period | Operating, investing, financing cash flows |

## 4. Balance Sheet
The Balance Sheet is built around:

`Assets = Liabilities + Equity`

It helps assess financial position, liquidity, financing structure, solvency/risk, and resource allocation.

## 5. Income Statement and Profit Levels

```text
Revenue
  - Cost of Sales
= Gross Profit
  - Operating Expenses
= Operating Profit
  - Other expenses including finance costs/taxes
= Net Profit
```

The Income Statement supports analysis of profitability, revenue generation, cost control, operational performance, and trends.

## 6. Cash Flow Statement

```text
Operating Activities → core business cash flows
Investing Activities → long-term asset/investment cash flows
Financing Activities → debt/equity funding cash flows
```

Cash flow is critical for liquidity and survival. A company can report profit yet still face failure if it lacks available cash.

## 7. Financial Ratios
The lecture introduces four ratio families:
- **Profitability:** ability to generate profit.
- **Liquidity:** ability to meet short-term obligations.
- **Efficiency:** effectiveness of resource/working-capital use.
- **Gearing:** debt versus equity financing and associated risk.

# Likely Exam Questions

1. **Define financial accounting.**  
   **Expected answer:** Recording, classifying, summarizing, and reporting business financial transactions to provide useful information for decision-making.

2. **Differentiate Revenue and Income.**  
   **Expected answer:** Revenue is total earnings before expenses; Income is what remains after expenses are deducted.

3. **Differentiate Expense and Expenditure.**  
   **Expected answer:** Expenses are recurring operating costs; Expenditure is all business spending, including investments such as equipment.

4. **State and rearrange the accounting equation.**  
   **Expected answer:** Assets = Liabilities + Equity; Equity = Assets - Liabilities; Liabilities = Assets - Equity.

5. **What does a Balance Sheet show?**  
   **Expected answer:** Financial position at a specific point in time through assets, liabilities, and equity.

6. **What does an Income Statement show?**  
   **Expected answer:** Financial performance over a period through revenue, expenses, and profit/loss.

7. **Calculate Gross Profit.**  
   **Expected answer:** Revenue - Cost of Sales.

8. **Calculate Operating Profit.**  
   **Expected answer:** Gross Profit - Operating Expenses.

9. **Name the three Cash Flow Statement activity categories.**  
   **Expected answer:** Operating, Investing, Financing.

10. **Why can a profitable company still fail?**  
    **Expected answer:** It may not have enough available cash to meet obligations despite reporting profit.

11. **Classify: customer receipts, equipment purchase, loan received.**  
    **Expected answer:** Operating, Investing, Financing.

12. **What do liquidity ratios measure?**  
    **Expected answer:** Ability to meet short-term obligations using short-term assets.

13. **What does a gearing ratio indicate?**  
    **Expected answer:** The relationship between debt and equity, financing structure, and financial risk.

14. **Interpret a high but manageable gearing level.**  
    **Expected answer:** The business relies significantly on debt, increasing risk, but debt may support expansion/growth.
