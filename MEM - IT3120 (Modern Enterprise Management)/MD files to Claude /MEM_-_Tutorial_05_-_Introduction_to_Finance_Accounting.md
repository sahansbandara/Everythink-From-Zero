## Question 1 - What is financial accounting?

**Answer:**

Financial accounting is the process of recording, classifying, summarizing, and reporting an organization's financial transactions through financial statements.

**Simple meaning:** It organizes business financial information so users can understand the company's financial performance and position.

### Exam Tip

Useful keywords:

```text
record
classify
summarize
report
financial transactions
financial statements
```

## Question 2 - Internal and External Users

### Internal Users

Two examples:

- Managers
- Employees

### External Users

Two examples:

- Investors / shareholders
- Banks / creditors

Other possible external users can include government authorities and suppliers, depending on the context.

### Simple Comparison

| Internal Users | External Users |
|---|---|
| Work within the organization | Are outside the organization |
| Use information for internal decisions | Use information to assess the organization |
| Managers | Investors |
| Employees | Banks / creditors |

## Question 3 - Three Key Financial Statements

**Answer:**

1. **Income Statement**
2. **Statement of Financial Position / Balance Sheet**
3. **Cash Flow Statement**

### Simple Meaning

| Statement | Main Purpose |
|---|---|
| Income Statement | Shows revenue, expenses, and profit over a period |
| Balance Sheet / Statement of Financial Position | Shows assets, liabilities, and equity at a point in time |
| Cash Flow Statement | Shows cash inflows and outflows |

### Common Mistake

Do not confuse the **Income Statement** with the **Cash Flow Statement**. Profit is not the same as cash movement.

## Question 4 - Accounting Equation

**Answer:**

```text
Assets = Liabilities + Equity
```

Rearrangements:

```text
Equity = Assets - Liabilities
Liabilities = Assets - Equity
```

### Definition

**Definition:** The accounting equation states that the resources owned by a business are financed either by liabilities or by owners' equity.

**Simple meaning:** Everything the business owns must have been financed by either debt or the owners.

## Question 7 - Revenue and Expenses

### Revenue

**Definition:** Revenue is income earned by a business from providing goods or services.

**Simple meaning:** Money/value earned from normal business activities.

### Expenses

**Definition:** Expenses are costs incurred in earning revenue and operating the business.

**Simple meaning:** Costs the business must bear to operate and generate income.

### Relationship

```text
Revenue - Expenses -> Profit
```

## Question 8 - Gross Profit and Operating Profit

Given:

```text
Revenue            = 150,000
Cost of Sales      = 90,000
Operating Expenses = 30,000
```

### Step 1 - Gross Profit

Formula:

```text
Gross Profit = Revenue - Cost of Sales
```

Calculation:

```text
Gross Profit = 150,000 - 90,000
             = 60,000
```

**Gross Profit = 60,000**

### Step 2 - Operating Profit

Formula:

```text
Operating Profit = Gross Profit - Operating Expenses
```

Calculation:

```text
Operating Profit = 60,000 - 30,000
                 = 30,000
```

**Operating Profit = 30,000**

### Flow

```text
Revenue                         150,000
- Cost of Sales                 (90,000)
----------------------------------------
Gross Profit                     60,000
- Operating Expenses            (30,000)
----------------------------------------
Operating Profit                 30,000
```

## Question 9 - Gross Profit vs Net Profit

### Gross Profit

Gross Profit is the amount remaining after deducting **Cost of Sales** from Revenue.

```text
Gross Profit = Revenue - Cost of Sales
```

### Net Profit

Net Profit is the final profit remaining after deducting the relevant operating and other expenses from revenue/profit.

### Comparison

| Gross Profit | Net Profit |
|---|---|
| Earlier profit measure | Final profit measure |
| Deducts Cost of Sales | Reflects additional expenses as well |
| Shows profitability from core selling activity before later expenses | Shows the overall final profit after expenses |

### Simple Meaning

```text
Gross Profit -> profit before many operating/other expenses
Net Profit   -> final profit after expenses
```

| Financing | Cash related to borrowing, repayment, and owner financing |

## Question 12 - Why Cash Flow Matters Even When Profitable

**Answer:**

A company can report a profit but still have insufficient cash available to pay:

- Employees.
- Suppliers.
- Loan obligations.
- Rent.
- Other short-term expenses.

For example, a company may record revenue from credit sales before customers actually pay the cash.

### Simple Meaning

```text
Profit on paper ≠ cash available now
```

### Exam Tip

Use the word **liquidity** when explaining why cash flow matters.

## Question 13 - Net Profit Margin

Given:

```text
Revenue    = 200,000
Net Profit = 40,000
```

Formula:

```text
Net Profit Margin = (Net Profit / Revenue) × 100
```

Calculation:

```text
Net Profit Margin = (40,000 / 200,000) × 100
                  = 0.20 × 100
                  = 20%
```

**Answer: 20%**

### Interpretation

For every LKR 100 of revenue, the company earns LKR 20 as net profit.

## Question 14 - Current Ratio

Given:

```text
Current Assets      = 90,000
Current Liabilities = 45,000
```

Formula:

```text
Current Ratio = Current Assets / Current Liabilities
```

Calculation:

```text
Current Ratio = 90,000 / 45,000
              = 2
```

**Answer: 2 : 1**

### Interpretation

The business has LKR 2 of current assets for every LKR 1 of current liabilities.

### Definition

**Definition:** The Current Ratio is a liquidity ratio comparing current assets with current liabilities.

**Simple meaning:** It indicates the business's ability to meet short-term obligations using short-term assets.

## Question 15 - High Debt Compared to Equity

**Answer:**

This is related to the **Debt-to-Equity Ratio**.

Formula:

```text
Debt-to-Equity Ratio = Total Debt / Total Equity
```

A high Debt-to-Equity Ratio indicates that the business relies heavily on debt financing compared with owners' equity.

It can indicate:

- Higher financial leverage.
- Greater repayment obligations.
- Potentially greater financial risk.

### Simple Meaning

```text
More debt relative to equity -> greater dependence on borrowed money
```

# Likely Exam Questions

### Question 1

State the accounting equation.

**Expected Answer:**

```text
Assets = Liabilities + Equity
```

### Question 2

Differentiate Assets, Liabilities, and Equity.

**Expected Answer:**

Assets are resources owned or controlled by the business, liabilities are obligations owed to others, and equity is the owners' residual interest after liabilities.

### Question 3

Differentiate Gross Profit and Operating Profit.

**Expected Answer:**

Gross Profit is Revenue minus Cost of Sales. Operating Profit is Gross Profit minus Operating Expenses.

### Question 4

Name the three cash flow activities.

**Expected Answer:**

Operating, Investing, and Financing activities.

### Question 5

Why can a profitable company still experience cash problems?

**Expected Answer:**

Profit is an accounting measure and does not guarantee that cash has already been received. The company may therefore lack liquidity to meet short-term obligations.

### Question 6

Calculate the Net Profit Margin if Net Profit is 40,000 and Revenue is 200,000.

**Expected Answer:**

```text
(40,000 / 200,000) × 100 = 20%
```

### Question 7

What does a Current Ratio measure?

**Expected Answer:**

It measures short-term liquidity by comparing Current Assets with Current Liabilities.

### Question 8

What does a high Debt-to-Equity Ratio indicate?

**Expected Answer:**

It indicates greater dependence on debt financing and potentially greater financial leverage and risk.

# Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Confusing financial accounting with cash management only.
- Naming more than the requested two internal/external users without identifying which group they belong to.
- Forgetting one of the three major financial statements.
- Writing the accounting equation backwards.
- Adding liabilities to assets when calculating equity.
- Classifying a bank loan as an asset because the company received cash from it.
- Classifying retained earnings as revenue rather than equity.
- Calculating Gross Profit after deducting Operating Expenses.
- Treating Gross Profit and Net Profit as identical.
- Classifying equipment purchases as Operating Activities.
- Classifying loan repayment as Investing Activities.
- Assuming profit means the company definitely has enough cash.
- Forgetting to multiply Net Profit Margin by `100`.
- Writing a Current Ratio of `2%` instead of `2:1`.
- Confusing Current Ratio with Debt-to-Equity Ratio.
- Interpreting high debt relative to equity as low leverage.

