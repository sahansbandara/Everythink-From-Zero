# IT3120 – Workshop 2: HRM – 16 Cases
## Summary + 2 Questions + Model Answers

> Answer format used throughout: **Claim → Because → Consequence**. That is the structure that earns full marks; a WHAT-only answer scores half.
> Highest marking sections: **Analysis 25% + Recommendations 25%**.

## 1. High Employee Turnover
**Summary:** Mid-sized Sri Lankan IT company loses experienced engineers within 1–2 years. Exit interviews cite higher salaries, better work-life balance and remote work at foreign companies. Result: project disruption, missed deadlines, heavy recruitment/training cost.

**Q1. Salary is a hygiene factor. If you only raise salaries, why will turnover likely return?**
> Raising salary removes **dissatisfaction** but does not create **satisfaction**, because under **Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory** pay is a hygiene factor while recognition, growth and responsibility are the actual motivators. The consequence is that employees stop complaining about pay but still leave once a competitor offers marginally more — the company pays permanently higher salaries and still loses staff.

**Q2. What does replacing one senior engineer actually cost?**
> The real cost is far above the salary line, because it includes **recruitment cost, onboarding time, lost productivity during ramp-up, the senior staff hours spent training the replacement, and the tacit project knowledge that left with the employee**. The consequence is that turnover directly converts into **missed deadlines and project disruption**, which is exactly what the case reports.

## 2. Poor Recruitment Decisions
**Summary:** Fast-growing IT company recruited developers rapidly for an international project, selecting on **academic qualifications rather than technical competence**. Recruits underperform → poor code quality, delays, extra load on seniors.

**Q1. At which stage did this fail — job analysis, sourcing, or selection?**
> The failure is at the **selection stage**, because the company used the wrong selection criterion (qualifications) instead of assessing the competencies the role actually requires. The consequence is a **person–job mismatch**: the recruits are hireable on paper but cannot deliver, producing poor code quality and delays.

**Q2. Trade-off of adding technical testing under time pressure?**
> Technical testing raises **selection validity** but lengthens the hiring cycle, because tests must be designed, administered and evaluated. The consequence is a short delay at hiring versus months of rework later — so the trade-off strongly favours testing, and can be reduced using **short practical coding tasks rather than long assessments**.

## 3. Low Employee Motivation
**Summary:** Employees complete assigned work but show no initiative or innovation. Little recognition for good performance; employees feel undervalued. Productivity and creativity have steadily declined.

**Q1. Which Herzberg motivators are missing, and why do hygiene fixes not solve it?**
> The missing motivators are **recognition, achievement, responsibility and advancement**, because the case states employees feel undervalued and receive no recognition. The consequence is that improving hygiene factors (pay, conditions) will produce compliance but not **discretionary effort** — employees will keep doing the minimum, which is precisely the reported symptom.

**Q2. Recognition is free — why hasn't management done it?**
> The likely cause is an **absent performance management system**, because managers cannot recognise performance they do not measure or review. The consequence is that recognition must be built into a process (regular reviews, peer recognition) rather than left to individual manager goodwill — otherwise it will not survive delivery pressure.

## 4. Ineffective Performance Management
**Summary:** Appraisals only **once a year** with minimal ongoing feedback. Employees unclear on expectations and career progression; high performers overlooked; poor performers unsupported.

**Q1. Why is annual-only appraisal a system failure, and what is the consequence for high performers?**
> It is a **system design failure**, because a 12-month feedback cycle cannot correct behaviour or acknowledge achievement close to when it happens. For high performers the consequence is that strong work goes unrecognised for up to a year, which removes the motivator of **recognition** and pushes the best staff toward exit — linking this case directly to Case 1 turnover.

**Q2. What new cost/risk does continuous feedback create for line managers?**
> Continuous feedback adds **managerial time and skill demand**, because managers must hold structured conversations regularly and document outcomes. The consequence is that without manager training the reviews become superficial box-ticking, so the recommendation must include **manager capability development**, not just a policy change.

## 5. Weak Reward System
**Summary:** Similar rewards given to high performers and average employees. Employees see the system as unfair; limited recognition, career development and performance-based incentives. Motivation and retention have declined significantly.

**Q1. Explain unfairness → motivation → retention. Which breaks first?**
> **Motivation breaks first**, because when effort and reward are not linked employees rationally reduce effort — there is no return on extra work. The consequence is that sustained demotivation converts into **retention loss**, as the highest performers (who lose most from equal rewards) are the ones with the strongest external options.

**Q2. Risk of performance-based pay in software teams, and how to control it?**
> The main risk is **individual incentives damaging team collaboration**, because developers optimise for personally measured output rather than shared delivery. The consequence is reduced code review, knowledge sharing and quality — controlled by using **mixed team-and-individual criteria** and non-financial recognition alongside incentives.

## 6. Leadership Issues in Agile Teams
**Summary:** Agile team led by a manager who decides everything alone and discourages participation. Daily stand-ups dominated by the leader → poor collaboration, low morale, slower decisions.

**Q1. Why is autocratic style specifically incompatible with Agile?**
> Agile depends on **self-organising teams and distributed decision-making**, because the team closest to the work holds the best information. An autocratic leader routes every decision through one person, so the consequence is a **decision bottleneck** — which is exactly the "slower decision-making" the case reports, despite Agile being designed for speed.

**Q2. Which style do you recommend, and what happens in a crisis?**
> Recommend a **participative / servant-leadership** style, because it restores team input and morale. However, in a genuine crisis participative decision-making is too slow, so the leader must be able to shift temporarily to **directive** mode — the consequence of not acknowledging this is a recommendation that fails under deadline pressure.

## 7. Conflict in Project Teams
**Summary:** Developers and QA engineers disagree about software quality and release schedules. Poor communication and weak collaboration raise conflict → missed deadlines, lower quality, reduced morale.

**Q1. Functional or dysfunctional conflict?**
> This is **dysfunctional conflict**, because the case states it produces missed deadlines, lower product quality and reduced morale — it damages outcomes rather than improving decisions. Some Dev–QA tension is naturally functional (it surfaces defects), so the consequence is that the goal is to **channel** the conflict, not eliminate it.

**Q2. Which conflict-handling approach, and its cost?**
> Recommend **collaborating**, because the underlying issue is a genuine goal conflict (speed vs quality) that needs a shared definition of "release ready." The cost is **time**: collaboration requires joint sessions and agreed quality criteria, so the consequence is short-term schedule investment to prevent repeated late-stage rework.

## 8. Lack of Skills Development
**Summary:** IT company adopted cloud computing and AI, but many employees lack required skills. Limited training leaves employees overwhelmed → lower productivity, project delays.

**Q1. What is TNA and when should it have happened?**
> **Training Needs Analysis (TNA)** is the systematic identification of the gap between required and current competencies at organisational, task and individual level. It should have run **before** the cloud/AI adoption decision was executed, because technology adoption creates the skill requirement. The consequence of skipping it is that the company committed to technologies its workforce could not operate — producing the reported delays.

**Q2. Train existing staff or hire new skilled staff?**
> Recommend **training as the primary route with selective hiring**, because training retains existing domain and product knowledge that new hires would take months to acquire. The trade-off is that training is slower to produce capability, so the consequence is a **hybrid approach**: hire a small number of experienced cloud/AI staff to lead, and upskill the rest around them.

## 9. Poor Communication
**Summary:** Employees unclear about roles and responsibilities. Inconsistent instructions from management and weak inter-team communication cause duplication of work and missed deadlines.

**Q1. Channels or role definition — which is the root cause?**
> The root cause is **role and responsibility definition**, because the case reports duplication of work, which happens when two people believe the same task is theirs — a structural clarity problem, not a message-delivery problem. The consequence is that adding communication tools alone will not fix it; the fix is **documented role clarity (e.g. a responsibility matrix)**.

**Q2. Why can "more meetings" worsen productivity?**
> More meetings consume **delivery time without resolving ambiguity**, because the inconsistent instructions originate from management alignment, not meeting frequency. The consequence is reduced coding time plus continued confusion — so the correct fix is **single-source, consistent written direction**, then fewer, better-structured meetings.

## 10. Personality Clashes in Teams
**Summary:** Team members have very different personalities and working styles. Introverts rarely contribute in meetings; outspoken members dominate. Collaboration and team effectiveness have reduced.

**Q1. What management failure turned diversity into a liability?**
> The failure is **unmanaged meeting facilitation**, because personality diversity only produces better decisions when every member's input actually reaches the discussion. Without facilitation, the loudest voice sets direction, and the consequence is that the team loses the analytical contribution of quieter members — reducing effectiveness despite having capable people.

**Q2. One concrete practice for introvert participation, and its downside?**
> Use **written input before the meeting** (agenda circulated, comments submitted in advance), because it separates thinking time from speaking time. The downside is added process overhead and slower spontaneity, so the consequence is that it should apply to **decision meetings, not daily stand-ups**.

## 11. Employee Burnout
**Summary:** Rapid growth; employees work overtime for months to meet aggressive deadlines. Reports of stress, fatigue, poor work-life balance, declining satisfaction. Absenteeism increased; several experienced employees intend to resign.

**Q1. Trace the chain and identify the cheapest intervention point.**
> The chain is **sustained overtime → stress and fatigue → declining job satisfaction → absenteeism → resignation intention → loss of experienced staff and delivery capacity**. The cheapest intervention is at the **workload-planning stage**, because preventing unrealistic deadline commitments costs planning discipline, whereas intervening after resignation costs full replacement and knowledge loss.

**Q2. The company is growing — justify why reducing overtime doesn't cut capacity.**
> Reducing overtime protects **sustainable capacity**, because fatigued engineers produce more defects and rework, and resigning staff remove capacity permanently. The consequence is that sustained overtime gives a short-term output gain followed by a larger capacity loss — so capacity should be added through **hiring and prioritisation**, not hours.

## 12. Managing a Hybrid Workforce
**Summary:** Hybrid model with some employees remote and some in office. Managers observe reduced collaboration, inconsistent communication, and difficulty maintaining engagement and team cohesion across locations.

**Q1. Technology problem or HRM/management practice problem?**
> It is an **HRM and management practice problem**, because the case reports engagement and cohesion issues, not tool failures — the same tools work in fully remote companies. The consequence is that buying more collaboration software will not fix it; the fix is **deliberate practices**: synchronised in-office days, consistent communication norms, and remote-inclusive meeting rules.

**Q2. If you mandate office days, which risk from Case 1 do you re-create?**
> You re-create the **turnover risk**, because Case 1 explicitly states employees are leaving for remote work opportunities at foreign companies. The consequence is that a rigid mandate solves cohesion at the cost of retention, so the recommendation must be a **limited, purpose-driven anchor day** rather than a full return-to-office.

## 13. Diversity and Inclusion Challenges
**Summary:** International software company with employees of different cultures, nationalities and generations. Misunderstandings from communication styles, cultural differences and unconscious bias affect teamwork and relationships. The company wants a more inclusive culture.

**Q1. Define unconscious bias and how one HR process spreads it.**
> **Unconscious bias** is an automatic, unintentional preference or judgement that influences decisions without the decision-maker's awareness. In a **promotion process** it causes managers to favour people similar to themselves, and the consequence is that the bias becomes **structural**: the leadership layer grows homogeneous, which then reproduces the same bias in every future decision.

**Q2. Why does diversity training alone fail, and what structural change would you add?**
> Training alone fails because it changes **awareness but not decision processes**, and awareness fades without reinforcement. The structural addition is **standardised, criteria-based decision-making** — structured interviews, documented promotion criteria and diverse panels — the consequence being that bias is constrained by process even when awareness lapses.

## 14. Resistance to Organizational Change
**Summary:** Company implementing a new project management system plus departmental restructuring. Employees resist because they fear increased workloads and job insecurity. Productivity has temporarily declined.

**Q1. Which change-management step was skipped, and the consequence?**
> The skipped step is **communication and participation before implementation**, because employees fear job insecurity — a fear that exists only when the purpose and job impact of the restructuring were never explained. The consequence is that employees fill the information gap with worst-case assumptions, converting uncertainty into active resistance and the reported productivity decline.

**Q2. How would you know the decline is temporary rather than permanent?**
> Track **productivity trend against the adoption curve** — output should fall during transition and recover as competence with the new system builds. The consequence of no recovery within the expected learning period is that the problem is not transition friction but a **genuine system or workload defect**, requiring redesign rather than more change communication.

## 15. Knowledge Sharing and Employee Retention
**Summary:** Several senior engineers resigned, taking valuable technical knowledge. The organization has limited documentation and no formal knowledge-sharing process. New employees take a long time to become productive; delivery is delayed.

**Q1. Tacit vs explicit knowledge — which was lost, and why can't documentation recover it?**
> **Explicit knowledge** is codifiable (documents, code, procedures); **tacit knowledge** is experience-based judgement — why a design decision was made, where the system breaks, how to debug it. What left was largely **tacit**, and the consequence is that documentation alone cannot recover it because tacit knowledge transfers through **interaction**: mentoring, pairing, shadowing and handover periods.

**Q2. Fix turnover (Case 1) or knowledge sharing first — justify sequencing.**
> Fix **knowledge sharing first**, because it is faster to implement and immediately reduces the damage of each future departure, whereas retention improvements take months to show effect. The consequence of the reverse order is that the company keeps losing knowledge while waiting for retention initiatives to work — so knowledge management acts as the **risk control** while retention is being addressed.

## 16. Ethical Issues in HRM
**Summary:** Complaints about favoritism in promotions, inconsistent disciplinary actions and unequal access to training. Employees perceive HR decisions as unfair → declining trust, lower morale, concerns about organizational ethics and transparency.

**Q1. Why does perceived unfairness alone damage the organization?**
> Because employees act on **perception, not on management's intent** — if effort appears unrelated to reward, the rational response is to reduce effort or exit. The consequence chain is **perceived unfairness → loss of trust → lower morale and discretionary effort → disengagement and turnover**, meaning the organisation suffers real damage even if every decision was defensible.

**Q2. Which HR process would you make transparent first, and what resistance?**
> Make **promotion criteria** transparent first, because promotion is the decision employees most strongly associate with favouritism and it signals what the organisation actually values. Expect resistance from **managers who lose discretion** and must now justify decisions against published criteria — the consequence being that the change needs senior-management enforcement, not HR advocacy alone.

## 🎯 Universal Fallback Q&A

| Question to ask | Model answer core |
|---|---|
| "What is the consequence on project delivery?" | Every HRM failure ends in one of: **delays, quality loss, cost increase, or knowledge loss**. |
| "Which theory applies — Herzberg, Maslow, TNA?" | Motivation/reward cases → **Herzberg**; needs hierarchy → **Maslow**; skills gap → **TNA**. |
| "Short-term vs long-term recommendation?" | Short = process/communication fix (weeks); long = culture, capability, reward redesign (months). |
| "Why is this IT-specific?" | IT work is **knowledge-intensive, project-deadline-driven, and skills obsolete fast** — so turnover and skill gaps hurt more than in labour-intensive industries. |
| "How would you know it failed?" | Name a metric: turnover rate, absenteeism, defect rate, time-to-productivity, engagement score. |

## 🧠 Trick

**Case blocks (4×4):** **R–M–T–O** → **R**ecruit/Retain (1–4) · **M**otivate/Manage (5–8) · **T**eam (9–12) · **O**rganizational (13–16)

**Herzberg trap:**
> Salary, policy, conditions = **hygiene** → removes dissatisfaction only
> Recognition, growth, responsibility, achievement = **motivators** → creates satisfaction
> ⚠ Cases **1, 3, 5** all fail because a **motivator problem** is treated with a **hygiene fix**.
